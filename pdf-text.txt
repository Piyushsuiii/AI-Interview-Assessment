ABSTRACT
Recruitment requires organizations to assess candidates consistently and make decisions that can
be explained. Conventional workflows often divide resumes, interviews, coding exercises,
reviewer notes, and reports across disconnected tools. The AI Hiring Intelligence Platform was
developed as a unified, multi-tenant web application for structured technical hiring. It allows
recruiters to define jobs and skills, publish versioned assessments, invite candidates, conduct
adaptive interviews, evaluate coding and system-design work, and generate evidence-linked
reports. A separate candidate portal provides passwordless access, saved progress, resume
management, and privacy-request functions.
Resume intelligence is provided by CVerify, a locally served DistilBERT classifier covering 24
job families. On a held-out test set, it achieved 86.35% accuracy, 85.36% weighted F1-score, and
79.47% macro F1-score.
The platform uses Next.js, NestJS, PostgreSQL, Redis, FastAPI, and restricted Docker
containers. Security controls include separate recruiter and candidate sessions, role-based
permissions, organization-scoped data access, hashed tokens, immutable assessment snapshots,
and audit records. Artificial intelligence supports rather than replaces professional judgement:
outputs are validated, evidence is retained, and recommendations remain reviewable. The project
improves workflow consistency and traceability while recognizing the need for human oversight,
privacy governance, bias monitoring, and field validation.
Keywords: artificial intelligence, recruitment, adaptive interview, resume classification,
DistilBERT, candidate assessment, explainable evaluation.

CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
1.1. Client Identification/Need Identification/Identification of Relevant Contemporary Issue
The principal clients of this project are recruitment teams, hiring managers, and technical
interviewers in organizations that recruit candidates for skill-intensive roles. These users must
define job requirements, process applications, coordinate assessments, compare evidence, and
document decisions. Candidates are an equally important stakeholder group because they provide
sensitive information, complete the assessment, and experience the consequences of the process.
System administrators form a third group responsible for access, security, service availability,
and usage governance.
The need exists because hiring requirements and available skills are changing rapidly. The World
Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2025, based on a survey of more than 1,000 employers
representing over 14 million workers across 55 economies, reports that 39% of workers’ existing
skill sets are expected to be transformed or become outdated by 2030. It also identifies skills gaps
as a major barrier to business transformation and shows that artificial intelligence and
information-processing technologies are expected to reshape many organizations. These findings
create pressure to assess demonstrable capability and adaptability rather than depend only on job
titles and resume keywords.
The current issue is also procedural. Technical recruitment may involve resume screening,
conversational interviews, coding exercises, system-design tasks, reviewer notes, and final
reports. When these activities occur in disconnected tools, information must be copied manually,
assessment criteria can vary, and the reasoning behind a decision becomes difficult to
reconstruct. Research on personnel selection has shown that job-related structure and
standardized evaluation improve the quality and consistency of interviews. At the same time,
algorithmic hiring studies warn that poorly validated data and models may reproduce
discrimination or hide unsuitable assumptions.
This is a contemporary issue recognized by major institutions. NIST provides an AI
risk-management framework based on governance, mapping, measurement, and management.
The European Union AI Act identifies specified employment-related AI applications as high-risk
and emphasizes risk controls, documentation, human oversight, and performance. The identified
need is therefore not simply faster automation. Clients require an integrated process that is
structured, traceable, secure, candidate-aware, and subject to responsible human control.

1.2. Identification of Problem
Technical recruitment depends on several forms of information that are difficult to collect and
compare consistently. A resume describes previous experience but does not directly demonstrate
current coding, analytical, communication, or system-design ability. An interview may reveal
reasoning, yet two candidates can receive different questions, prompts, and levels of assistance.
A coding result can indicate whether tests passed but may omit the candidate’s explanation and
the context in which the task was completed. Recruiters must combine these fragments before
reaching a decision, often under time pressure and with incomplete traceability.
The broad problem is the absence of a coherent and accountable assessment process that connects
job requirements with comparable candidate evidence. Fragmented workflows lead to repeated
data entry and make it difficult to establish which job skill was assessed by a question, which
version of that question was presented, what the candidate submitted, and how the evidence
affected the final judgement. If assessment content changes during recruitment, candidates may
unknowingly be compared under different conditions. If criteria are not defined in advance,
reviewers may place inconsistent weight on similar answers.
A second aspect is limited evidence visibility. Numerical scores and short summaries can appear
precise while concealing the assumptions, source material, or uncertainty behind them. This risk
becomes greater when artificial intelligence participates in screening or evaluation. A fluent
automated explanation may be accepted without checking whether it is grounded in the
candidate’s actual response. Historical data, proxy variables, unbalanced datasets, and unsuitable
labels can also produce unequal outcomes even when protected characteristics are not explicitly
used.
The problem also affects candidates. Invitation links, interview progress, resume records,
consent, and privacy requests may be distributed across different channels. Connection failures
can cause work to be lost, while unclear data practices can reduce trust. Recruitment data
includes identity details, employment history, written answers, source code, and evaluative
conclusions, all of which require controlled access and retention.
The problem can therefore be stated as follows: technical hiring lacks a unified, consistent,
evidence-oriented, and governable mechanism for relating role requirements to candidate
assessment data while preserving comparability, privacy, security, reviewability, and human
accountability. This definition intentionally describes the need and its boundaries without
prescribing a particular technical solution.

CHAPTER 2
LITERATURE REVIEW/BACKGROUND STUDY
2.1. Timeline of the Reported Problem
The difficulty of making consistent hiring decisions existed before digital recruitment. Early
personnel selection often depended on unstructured interviews and individual judgement. During
the twentieth century, industrial and organizational psychology developed standardized tests, job
analysis, and structured interview methods. By the 1990s, research had documented specific
components of interview structure, including consistent questions, controlled prompting,
anchored ratings, and interviewer preparation. Schmidt and Hunter later synthesized decades of
evidence and showed why selection methods must be evaluated by demonstrated validity rather
than intuitive confidence.
The spread of the internet in the late 1990s and 2000s moved vacancies and applications online.
Applicant tracking systems improved storage, search, and workflow management, but keyword
filtering could not establish practical competence and often retained opaque screening rules.
Online testing and coding platforms subsequently allowed employers to collect work samples at
scale. These tools improved access to task evidence but frequently remained separate from
interviews, applicant records, and final reviewer notes.
During the 2010s, machine learning expanded from search and ranking into resume matching,
candidate engagement, and assessment. Transformer architectures became a major turning point.
BERT demonstrated effective bidirectional language pre-training for downstream text tasks;
DistilBERT later reduced model size and inference cost through knowledge distillation. These
developments made local resume classification and large-scale language analysis more practical.
At the same time, reported risks became more visible. Applicant-reaction research showed that
digital selection must be judged by procedural justice and candidate experience as well as
organizational efficiency. Systematic reviews documented how data, labels, features, and
deployment choices can cause discrimination. Raghavan et al. found that bias-mitigation claims
in algorithmic hiring often used inconsistent definitions and limited external validation.
From 2020 onward, generative AI increased the ability to create questions, summarize answers,
and support conversational assessment, while also increasing concerns about hallucination and
over-reliance. Governance consequently became central: NIST released AI RMF 1.0 in 2023, the
EU adopted the AI Act in 2024, and employment authorities issued guidance on adverse impact.
The historical pattern shows a progression from informal judgement to structured assessment,
digital workflow, algorithmic assistance, and finally governed, human-supervised AI.

2.2. Proposed Solutions
Researchers and practitioners have proposed several classes of solution to recruitment problems.
The first is the structured interview. Candidates receive job-related questions under consistent
conditions, and evaluators use predetermined criteria or anchored rating scales. This method
improves comparability and reduces some sources of interviewer discretion. Its limitation is that
a strict script may not adequately investigate an unusual, incomplete, or highly promising
response.
The second class is the applicant tracking and resume-screening system. Such systems
centralize vacancies and applications, support search, and automate status changes.
Natural-language processing can extend keyword matching by classifying documents or
estimating semantic similarity. BERT and DistilBERT provide strong foundations for focused
text classification. However, resume language is an indirect and socially influenced
representation of competence. Classification accuracy can hide weak minority-class performance,
and historical patterns may introduce proxy bias. Resume output should therefore organize
information rather than determine employability.
The third solution is online practical assessment. Coding platforms run test cases against
submitted programs, while work-sample and system-design tasks collect direct evidence. These
methods are more closely related to job activity than resume keywords, but secure execution
requires isolation, time and memory limits, hidden-test protection, and resistance to service
misuse. A test result also needs context because passing cases does not fully explain
maintainability, reasoning, or trade-offs.
The fourth solution is AI-assisted recruitment. Language models can analyze roles, suggest
competencies, generate questions, ask follow-ups, and summarize evidence. Black and van Esch
describe opportunities across sourcing, screening, engagement, and selection, while Tambe et al.
emphasize data, organizational, and ethical challenges in human-resource AI. The main
drawbacks are unverifiable output, prompt sensitivity, provider dependence, privacy exposure,
and automation bias.
The fifth solution is responsible-AI governance. Human review, model documentation, audit
records, output validation, bias monitoring, candidate notice, and appeal processes address risks
that model performance alone cannot resolve. Governance is essential but does not itself create
an usable assessment workflow. The literature therefore supports an integrated approach:
structured criteria, practical evidence, bounded AI assistance, candidate-centred access, secure
engineering, and a final decision retained by accountable people.

2.4. Review Summary
The literature establishes that recruitment quality depends on job relevance, consistency,
evidence, and accountable judgement. Structured interviews provide a strong foundation because
comparable questions and defined scoring guidance reduce uncontrolled variation.
Digital-selection research adds that effectiveness cannot be measured only by recruiter speed.
Candidates judge the process through clarity, opportunity to perform, job relatedness, treatment,
and the ability to understand what is expected.
Natural-language processing expands the available technical methods. Transformer models can
classify resume text and assist analysis, while generative models can draft role-aligned content
and summarize responses. However, language fluency is not evidence of factual correctness or
predictive validity. Accuracy can also conceal class imbalance, and a resume category cannot
establish an individual’s competence. Therefore, automated outputs need bounded purpose,
measured performance, clear uncertainty, and supporting source material.
The fairness literature provides the strongest warning against a purely automated approach. Bias
can enter through collection practices, historical decisions, proxy features, labels, objective
functions, and deployment choices. Removing a protected field does not remove all correlated
information. Governance consequently requires representative evaluation, subgroup monitoring,
documentation, access controls, candidate communication, human review, and a method for
questioning or changing an automated recommendation.
These findings connect directly to the project. A useful system should relate role skills to
versioned questions and preserve the candidate’s text, code, design, and test results as separate
evidence. Resume classification should support organization of documents, not automatic
rejection. Adaptive questioning should remain bounded so that individual clarification does not
destroy comparability. AI evaluations should follow validated structures and cite retained
evidence. Candidate and recruiter identities should be separated, sensitive internal material
should not be exposed, and untrusted code should not run inside the main application service.
The review reveals that individual solutions exist, but complete traceability across the hiring
workflow remains a gap. This project addresses that gap by integrating structured assessment,
practical tasks, resume intelligence, candidate self-service, AI-assisted evaluation, and human
review within one controlled architecture. It does not claim that integration proves fairness or
job-performance validity. Those claims require organizational pilots, representative participants,
adverse-impact analysis, accessibility evaluation, and longitudinal outcome data.

2.5. Problem Definition
The problem is to create an integrated technical-hiring environment that connects job
requirements, candidate information, structured assessment, practical work, and reviewable
evaluation without allowing automation to become an unsupported employment decision. The
environment must serve multiple organizations while preventing one organization from accessing
another organization’s jobs, candidates, assessments, interviews, or reports. It must also maintain
a distinct candidate identity boundary so that applicants cannot access recruiter-only scoring
criteria, hidden tests, integrity records, recommendations, or credentials.
The work is to be performed through a modular web architecture. Recruiters define jobs and
competencies, create versioned questions, publish assessments, invite candidates, and inspect
evidence-linked reports. Candidates use a separate portal to manage appropriate personal
information, access invitations, provide consent, complete resumable interviews, submit code and
system-design work, and make privacy requests. A relational database maintains authoritative
state and immutable assessment records. Background processing separates long-running or risky
activity from normal API requests. Untrusted code is executed only in restricted containers with
network and resource controls.
Artificial intelligence is applied only to defined support functions. A local DistilBERT service
classifies resume PDFs into 24 job families. Language-model providers assist role analysis,
assessment content, bounded follow-up generation, and structured evaluation. Provider outputs
are validated before persistence, failures produce safe fallback behaviour, and evaluation findings
are linked to stored candidate evidence. Model, provider, prompt, confidence, and usage
information are retained where relevant. Human recruiters review the resulting information and
remain responsible for the decision.
The project will not infer personality, honesty, emotion, protected characteristics, or future
performance from a person’s face or voice. It will not automatically issue offers or rejections,
claim universal legal compliance, expose hidden coding tests, or present resume classification as
proof of suitability. Video interviews, biometric emotion analysis, production cloud
orchestration, production-scale observability, and longitudinal employment-validity studies are
outside the present implementation.
Success is defined by an operational and testable workflow, correct access boundaries,
recoverable interview state, secure practical assessment, measured classifier results, traceable
evaluations, and explicit limitations. Fairness and legal compliance remain continuing
organizational responsibilities requiring representative data, policy, monitoring, and
jurisdiction-specific review.

The reported stack includes Next.js, NestJS, PostgreSQL, Redis, FastAPI, and Docker. External AI providers may
impose availability, latency, cost, and rate limits. The local resume classifier reduces dependence on a remote provider
for that classification task. Code execution is treated as untrusted workload and is separated from the primary API.
Economic constraints
The project must balance development time, hosting resources, model inference cost, storage, and test infrastructure.
Queue-based processing and provider abstraction help manage variable workloads. A production deployment would
require a separate operational budget for monitoring, backups, secure object storage, and availability.
Ethical, social, and legal constraints
Recruitment decisions affect employment opportunities. Automated outputs must be job-related, reviewable, and
subject to human judgement. Candidate communication, accessibility, privacy, bias monitoring, and correction
processes matter. Applicable employment, privacy, accessibility, and AI laws vary by jurisdiction; technical controls
alone do not establish legal compliance.

3.9 Algorithms and Processing Logic
Adaptive interview flow
The interview engine uses role context, the current question, and the candidate response to determine whether a
bounded clarification or follow-up is useful. Generated output is validated against an expected schema. The process is
constrained by the assessment plan so adaptation does not become unlimited or unrelated.
Input: role context, published assessment snapshot, current question, candidate response
1. Store the response against the active interview session.
2. Retrieve the question rubric and mapped competency.
3. Request a follow-up only when clarification or deeper evidence is useful.
4. Validate the generated question against schema, scope, and follow-up limits.
5. If generation fails or output is invalid, use the configured safe fallback.
6. Store the next state and continue.
Resume classification
CVerify extracts text from a PDF resume and passes the text to a locally served DistilBERT classifier. The model
assigns one of 24 job-family labels. Its output is an organizational aid, not a standalone hiring decision. Performance is
reported using held-out accuracy, weighted F1, and macro F1.
Evidence-linked evaluation
The evaluation process gathers candidate answers, code submissions, system-design material, and test outcomes. A
provider-flexible AI service returns structured findings such as competency scores, confidence, strengths, weaknesses,
and recommendations. The application validates the structure and associates findings with stored evidence. Reviewers
can inspect the source material and record a reason when overriding a recommendation.
Restricted code execution
Supported code is sent to a separate worker and run in a short-lived Docker container. The design specifies disabled
networking, restricted resources, and hard timeouts. Hidden tests and internal execution details remain outside the
candidate-facing interface. Containerization reduces exposure but does not eliminate every sandbox risk; production
use requires hardening and security review.

Recruitment workflow challenges
Recruitment teams may receive many resumes for a role and must identify relevant candidates, coordinate interview availability,
communicate access instructions and maintain consistent evaluation records. When these activities are spread across email,

and recruiter-facing operations in one application. The platform is intended to support a structured workflow rather than make an
autonomous hiring decision.
Expected value
Centralised records improve traceability. Automated resume classification can help organise incoming profiles. A candidate portal
reduces repeated manual communication. A queue-based worker can process background tasks without blocking interactive
requests. Human recruiters remain responsible for reviewing evidence and making hiring decisions.

E.17 AI Output Interpretation and Fairness
Decision support, not automatic hiring
Classification should support recruiter triage and not be used as the sole basis for rejection or selection. A job-family label does not

Evaluation considerations

review are valuable additions.
Bias and governance
Resume datasets can encode historical hiring bias. The team should review training data provenance, label quality, class balance
and sensitive-feature leakage. Model updates should be versioned and evaluated before release.

E.18 Background Jobs and BullMQ
Why use a worker
Background processing keeps long-running work away from latency-sensitive HTTP requests. The project notes identify BullMQ
with Redis and a worker concurrency setting of two. Queue jobs may include classification, notifications or other asynchronous
tasks, depending on the actual job registry.
Job lifecycle
A producer adds a job to the queue. A worker claims it, performs the task and reports completion or failure. Retry policy, backoff
and job retention should be configured to avoid uncontrolled repeated work or unbounded queue growth.
Concurrency and safety
Concurrency two means the worker is configured to process up to two jobs concurrently in the described setup. It does not by itself
establish throughput or production capacity. Jobs should be idempotent where possible so retries do not send duplicate emails or
create duplicate records.

E.44 Glossary and Abbreviations
Application terms
API: Application Programming Interface. E2E: End-to-End. UI: User Interface. OAuth: Open Authorisation. JWT: JSON Web
Token. OCR: Optical Character Recognition. URL: Uniform Resource Locator.
Platform terms
Prisma: database ORM used by the backend. PostgreSQL: relational database. Redis: in-memory data store used here for queue
coordination. BullMQ: Redis-backed job queue. Supabase Storage: object storage service used for private resume files.
AI and testing terms
DistilBERT: compact transformer language model. CVerify: project resume classification component. Precision: fraction of
predicted positives that are correct. Recall: fraction of actual positives found. F1: harmonic mean of precision and recall.
Playwright: browser automation framework. Jest: JavaScript testing framework.
